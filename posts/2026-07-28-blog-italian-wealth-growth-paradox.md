# Why a country full of rich households stopped getting more productive

I just handed in my final dissertation for my degree in Business and Economics at Bologna, and I want to write down what it was actually about before the whole thing blurs in my head. The title is *The Italian Wealth-Growth Paradox: How Household Asset Allocation and Structural Rigidities Hinder Productivity*, which is a mouthful, so here is the short version.

## The thing that bothered me

Italian households are rich. By the end of 2023 they held about EUR 11,286 billion in net wealth, the highest number in the Istat and Banca d'Italia series. At the end of 2022 that came to roughly 8.1 times gross disposable income, near the top of the OECD.

And yet productivity has gone nowhere. Total factor productivity slid from about 85 per cent of the US level in 1995 to around 70 per cent by 2018, and labour productivity grew by an average of about 0.3 per cent a year between 1995 and 2016.

That combination is not supposed to happen. Solow says that if you keep piling up capital, output per worker rises. Italy piled up capital for thirty years and output per worker basically sat still. Either the model is wrong or something is going on with the capital itself. I picked the second option and spent a dissertation on it.

## The argument

My claim is that the problem is the composition of Italian wealth, not the amount. Roughly two thirds of gross household wealth is non-financial, which is to say real estate. The rest leans heavily on bank deposits and government bonds. For the bottom half of households, over 90 per cent sits in dwellings and deposits.

Every one of those choices is sensible for the household making it. Housing is familiar, deposits are safe, BTPs feel patriotic and pay a coupon. In aggregate, though, it starves firms of the one thing that funds risky, long-horizon investment: equity.

I traced it as a chain with four links.

Households allocate defensively. That saving flows into banks rather than equity markets. Banks lend against collateral, which leaves innovative firms with an equity gap that the ECB puts at about 4 per cent of GDP for SMEs. And a firm that cannot raise equity stays small, which shows up in the data as capital misallocation, exactly the Hsieh and Klenow mechanism where scattered marginal products of capital read as weak TFP.

Some supporting numbers that stuck with me: Italian equity market capitalisation is about 28.6 per cent of GDP, venture capital about 0.36 per cent of GDP, and firms get roughly 70 per cent of external finance from banks.

## The method, and why I was careful about it

I did mechanism tracing rather than a regression. That means I set out to show each link is present, separately evidenced, and consistent with the others, and then I said plainly what that cannot do. It cannot rule out reverse causation. Weak growth prospects could be pushing households into safe assets rather than the other way round. It cannot rule out something like ageing driving both saving and stagnation. And it cannot tell you what share of the productivity gap this channel accounts for.

I graded my own links in a table, which felt slightly masochistic but made the argument honest. Links one and two are strong. Links three and four are moderate, because the gap and the misallocation are both real and measured for Italy, but I cannot partition them cleanly by cause.

## The finding I care about most

The 2014 Jobs Act turned out to be the useful natural experiment. Ciminelli and Franco find it raised productivity by about one percentage point a year inside the firms it actually touched. Real effect, cleanly identified. And the national productivity number did not move.

That contrast is the centre of my dissertation. The reform worked where it landed and it landed on firms above 15 employees, which in Italy means it skipped most of the economy. Labour rigidity was a genuine problem, but it was not the binding aggregate constraint. Something else is holding the line, and the financing structure of the micro-firm sector is my candidate.

The PNRR, all EUR 191.5 billion of it, is the first serious attempt at the capital side, and its orientation is right. But it works through public investment. It does not change how households hold their money, it does not deepen the equity market, and and it does not close the equity gap facing micro-firms. It gets partway along the chain and then stops.

## Where I landed

I was tempted to write a bigger conclusion than the evidence allowed, and my main discipline through the whole project was not doing that. I do not claim this channel beats the rival explanations. Pellegrino and Zingales on family management and the IT revolution is a strong account, and I ended up treating it as complementary rather than competing, since the family firm that will not hire an outside manager is the same firm that will not sell equity to an outsider. What I do claim is that the channel is real, measurable, coherent, and underweighted in a debate that has been fixed on labour flexibility for a decade.

The optimistic note, if there is one: Italy's problem is a mismatch, not a shortage. The savings exist. They are just parked in the wrong place. That is fixable in principle, which is more than a lot of countries can say. Whether a country this attached to property and deposits will vote for the fix is a different question and not one I can answer with Penn World Table data.

## What I would do next

Two designs would turn the consistency I showed into something causal, and I would like to run at least one of them.

The first links SHIW household portfolio records to firm-level productivity at the regional level, then tests whether regions with more defensive allocation see weaker productivity later. That is the direct test of my chain.

The second uses the PNRR itself. Disbursement varies across regions and sectors, and that variation can be used to estimate what a capital-side intervention actually does to firm financing and productivity. The spending is happening right now, so the data is arriving whether anyone studies it or not.

If I do a master's, one of those is my thesis. For now I am glad the thing is submitted.
