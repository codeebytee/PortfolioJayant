# Five Confident Numbers the Data Does Not Contain

Black–Scholes gives you one volatility. The market gives you a different one at
every strike and every maturity. Heston (1993) is the standard first answer to
that mismatch: make variance itself a mean-reverting stochastic process,
correlated with the spot, and let five parameters generate skew and term
structure at once.

```
dF_t = √v_t · F_t dW¹
dv_t = κ(θ − v_t) dt + σ_v √v_t dW²      d⟨W¹,W²⟩ = ρ dt
```

Fit κ, θ, σ_v, ρ, v₀ to this morning's chain and you have an
arbitrage-consistent model that prices the strikes nobody quotes and produces
Greeks that agree across the book. That's the pitch, and it is a good one.

I built the whole thing — three independent pricing engines, a real calibration
to a live SPY chain — and then asked the question the tutorials skip: **how much
should I trust the five numbers that come out?**

The answer, for a single expiry, is: not at all.

---

## The result

I ran L-BFGS-B sixty times from random starting points, log-uniform in κ and
σ_v, against one 14-day SPY smile — 161 quotes. Then I counted the restarts
landing within 2% of the best loss found. Two percent of loss is a gap far
smaller than the bid–ask spread on any of the options involved, so those fits
are not distinguishable using market data. Fifty-four of sixty qualified.

Here is the spread of parameters across those 54 indistinguishable fits, against
the same experiment run on all six expiries at once:

| | one expiry (14d) | six expiries |
|---|---|---|
| restarts within 2% of best | 54 / 60 | 59 / 60 |
| **κ** | 0.31 – 11.38 (**37×**) | 1.76 – 2.68 (1.5×) |
| **θ** | 0.005 – 0.299 (**60×**) | 0.058 – 0.072 (1.2×) |
| **κθ** | **441×** | 1.2× |
| **v₀** | 4.5× | 1.01× |
| **σ_v** | 1.42× | 1.04× |
| **ρ** | all railed at −0.98 | −0.7356 – −0.7283 |

Parameter sets whose mean-reversion speed differs by a factor of 37 reproduce
the same 161 market quotes to within half a vol point of each other. Overlay the
smiles they generate and you see one curve.

So reporting "κ = 2.3" off a front-month calibration is reporting a number the
data does not contain. Anyone hedging vega with a κ re-fit each morning from the
front month is hedging estimation noise.

## Why it happens, and why it isn't a broken optimiser

The split between what is and isn't identified turns out to be structural, and
once you see it the result stops being surprising.

A single maturity shows you the shape of **one** terminal density. Its asymmetry
fixes ρ. Its excess kurtosis fixes σ_v. And indeed those are the two parameters
that stay put across all 54 fits.

κ, θ and v₀ enter only through how that density *evolves* — and one expiry
contains no evolution. Many different mean-reversion stories arrive at the same
total variance on a single date. The optimiser is not failing; it is correctly
reporting that the data does not discriminate.

The loss surfaces show the same thing geometrically. Slice through the optimum
in (κ, σ_v) and the minimum lies along a long curved valley of near-constant
σ_v²/κ — the stationary variance of the CIR process. Slice in (κ, θ) and it runs
along near-constant κθ, the drift of variance toward its mean. **Those
combinations are what option prices see. Their individual factors are not.**

Two caveats I want to keep attached to this result, because they cut both ways:

- ρ looking perfectly determined on the 14-day slice is partly an artefact. Every
  one of those fits is *railed* at the −0.98 search bound. What looks like sharp
  identification is the optimiser running out of room.
- Six expiries collapse the family but do not eliminate it. κ still spans
  1.76–2.68 among fits that are indistinguishable in loss. The term structure —
  not a better optimiser, not cleaner data — is what buys identification, and
  even a full surface leaves κ good to maybe ±20%.

That ±20% matches an independent check. Generating a synthetic 240-quote chain
from known parameters, degrading it with tick rounding and ~0.15 vol points of
noise, then re-fitting: θ comes back to +0.03% and ρ to −0.05%, while κ is off
by **16%** and σ_v by 8%. From clean data generated by the model itself. The
mean-reversion parameters are the weakly determined ones even under ideal
conditions.

## The pricing underneath it

None of the above means anything if the pricer is wrong, so the larger half of
the project is making sure it isn't.

**Three independent engines.** COS (Fang & Oosterlee), Carr–Madan FFT, and
Andersen QE Monte Carlo. COS and the FFT agree to 8.9e-4 — that residual is the
FFT's log-strike interpolation error, not a disagreement about the model. Monte
Carlo sits within about one standard error at every strike, and matters because
it is the only one of the three that doesn't share the characteristic function.
Agreement with MC tests the CF itself.

Run times per 7-strike ladder: **COS 0.17 ms, FFT 0.68 ms, MC 9.9 s.** A factor
of 60,000, which is the entire argument for putting COS in the optimiser's inner
loop and keeping the other two as witnesses.

**The little Heston trap.** Heston's original paper writes the characteristic
function with `g₁ = (b+d)/(b−d)` and `e^{+dT}`. Algebraically identical to the
`g₂ = (b−d)/(b+d)`, `e^{−dT}` form. Not identical as *computed* numbers:
`numpy.log` returns the principal branch, the g₁ argument spirals around the
origin as T or σ_v grows, and the computed price jumps by a factor of
`e^{2πi·κθ/σ_v²}` at a maturity that depends on the parameters. With g₂,
`|g₂| ≤ 1` and `|e^{−dT}| ≤ 1`, so the log argument never leaves the right
half-plane and the principal branch is correct everywhere. The repo implements
only g₂ — and keeps a deliberately broken g₁ version around so a test can
demonstrate it failing.

**Three numerical corners that produce silently wrong answers.** Each of these
cost me real time:

1. *The COS truncation range needs a variance floor the paper doesn't have.* The
   closed form for the second cumulant is a long alternating expression, and for
   some parameter/maturity combinations the terms nearly cancel. At one
   perfectly ordinary parameter set the reported c₂ collapses near T = 4: the
   interval narrows from 6.2 wide to 1.9, most of the density falls outside it,
   and the ATM price drops 20% before recovering at T = 4.25. A notch in a term
   structure looks exactly like a branch discontinuity — an easy bug to
   misattribute. The fix is a rigorous lower bound from
   `Var(X_T) ≥ E[∫v dt]`, which is closed form, not padding.
2. *The call payoff coefficients overflow at extreme parameters.* They carry a
   factor `e^b`, and b grows like σ_v√T. At ten years with σ_v > 1 the pricer
   returns a call worth more than the forward. Put coefficients stay bounded, so
   past a width threshold the code prices the put and converts by parity.
3. *Carr–Madan's damping parameter must be clipped against a moment explosion.*
   Under Heston, `E[F_T^{α+1}]` blows up at a finite maturity that shrinks as ρ
   and σ_v grow. Leave α at the textbook 1.5 on a long-dated strongly-skewed fit
   and the FFT returns confident nonsense with no warning.

**One optimisation that made the study feasible.** In a naive COS pricer the
truncation interval depends on parameters through the cumulants, so the payoff
coefficients are rebuilt on every call — about 90% of the runtime. Pinning the
interval once, as the union over the 32 corners of the calibration box (so it is
never narrower than a per-call range would have been), makes each evaluation one
CF plus one matrix–vector product. Measured on the 901-quote chain: **12.1 ms →
1.8 ms per objective evaluation.** That's the difference between this study
taking four minutes and half an hour.

## What the fit actually says about SPY

Calibrated to 901 usable quotes across 6 expiries, SPY as of 2026-08-07 (2,126
listed contracts went in; **58% of the board is unusable** after two-sided
market, price floor, spread, open interest and moneyness filters — the funnel is
published):

```
κ = 2.049   θ = 0.0662   σ_v = 0.980   ρ = −0.730   v₀ = 0.0203
RMSE 2.03 vol points | 11.7 s | Feller ratio 0.282 → VIOLATED
```

v₀ = 0.0203 is spot vol of 14.3%, below the long-run θ → 25.7%: the market is
pricing a steeply rising term structure, calm now with reversion to a much
higher level. ρ = −0.73 is the usual deep negative equity skew.

The Feller ratio of 0.28 means the model's variance genuinely visits zero —
simulating the fitted parameters, 13.8% of (path, timestep) pairs sit at
numerically zero variance. I report that rather than constraining
2κθ ≥ σ_v², because enforcing Feller would refuse to fit the skew the market
actually shows. It is also why the simulation uses Andersen's QE rather than
Euler with a truncation patch.

**And the fit quality is entirely a maturity story:**

| days | n | RMSE (vol pts) | max abs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14 | 161 | 2.94 | 10.53 |
| 28 | 178 | 2.95 | 11.99 |
| 54 | 284 | 1.51 | 6.11 |
| 105 | 87 | 0.98 | 2.61 |
| 224 | 90 | 0.48 | 0.94 |
| 327 | 101 | 0.44 | 0.59 |

Beyond six months Heston fits to under half a vol point — inside the bid–ask
spread. At two weeks it is off by 2.9 points RMSE and up to 10.5 in the wings,
and the bias is negative: the model **underprices** the short-dated wings. Fit
the 14-day smile alone and ρ drives hard into the −0.98 bound and still leaves
3.79 points of RMSE.

Heston cannot make a two-week smile steep enough at any admissible parameters. A
continuous process needs time to accumulate that much asymmetry; only jumps
produce it that fast. That is the diffusive-model failure that motivates Bates
and rough volatility — measured here rather than asserted.

## The thing I'd tell someone starting this

The aggregate RMSE is a comforting number that hides both findings above. It
reports 2.03 vol points while the model is off by 12 on a two-week wing, and it
says nothing at all about the fact that κ is unidentified. In production the
monitors worth having are per-slice RMSE and a check on whether ρ has railed
against its bound — not the headline.

The right output of a Heston calibration is probably not a point estimate at
all. Given the identifiability result, it's a *set*: profile the loss and report
every parameter vector inside a spread-implied threshold. The 60-restart scatter
here is a diagnostic pointing at that, not a substitute for it.
